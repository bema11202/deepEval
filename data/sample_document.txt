# Healthcare Operations AI Trainer — Assessment Prep

For Bernard Masoko · Assessment Friday 11 September 2026

---

## A note on how this guide is built

There are deliberately **no ready-made example tasks** in here. Two reasons.

First, your instructions say submissions must be 100% your own and that
recycling supplied examples is disqualifying. If I hand you polished specimens,
the temptation to lean on them is real, and they'd be AI-generated — the exact
thing that gets people rejected.

Second, and more practically: the skill being assessed *is* generating these.
Reading someone else's finished examples builds recognition, not capability.
Working the framework builds capability.

So this gives you the analytical framework, verified current domain facts you
can check yourself, and drills that force you to produce. The output is yours.

---

## Part 1 — What's actually being tested

Read the email closely and four distinct competencies fall out:

1. **Task design** — inventing prompts that genuinely defeat a capable model
2. **Evaluation** — judging AI output against sources, with defensible reasoning
3. **Long-form grounded writing** — expert content that cites and holds together
4. **Instruction compliance** — doing exactly what's asked, to the letter

Most candidates fail on (4), not (1). The email says so directly. Attention
discipline beats brilliance here.

There's a fifth thing nobody states: **your writing must read as human.** Every
submission is reviewed by a person who reads these all day and has developed a
sharp ear for LLM prose. Part 6 covers this.

---

## Part 2 — How LLMs actually fail

You need a working model of model failure. These are the exploitable seams,
ordered by how well they fit your HIT/compliance background.

### 2.1 Temporal and status confusion — your strongest lever

Models compress "proposed," "final," "effective," and "enforced" into a mush of
"exists." Regulatory work lives on exactly those distinctions.

A model asked about the HIPAA Security Rule overhaul will often describe
mandatory encryption and MFA as current obligations. They are **proposed**, not
law — and the timeline has slipped twice (see Part 5). That gap between what a
model asserts and what's true is where a hard task lives.

### 2.2 Regulatory composition

Single-rule questions are easy. Questions where two rules interact — one
creating an exception the other narrows, or a federal floor a state law exceeds
— require holding several structures at once. Models flatten this.

### 2.3 Jurisdictional layering

Federal baseline plus state variation plus preemption analysis. Models default
to the federal answer and miss that a specific state imposes more. With 177
healthcare AI bills pending across 31 states, this vein is deep right now.

### 2.4 Citation fidelity

Models invent CFR sections, misattribute rule numbers, and confidently cite
Federal Register pages that don't exist. A task requiring exact citation to a
specific provision is hard in a way that's cleanly verifiable — either the cite
is right or it isn't.

### 2.5 Near-identical entity confusion

HTI-1, HTI-2, HTI-5. CMS-0057-F versus other CMS interoperability rules. TEFCA
versus the Manner Exception versus QHIN requirements. Models blur these
constantly because the names are so similar and co-occur in training data.

### 2.6 Negative knowledge

"What does this rule *not* cover?" "Which entity types are excluded?" Models are
trained toward helpful assertion, so they struggle to correctly enumerate
absences and often manufacture coverage that isn't there.

### 2.7 Refusing to speculate

Give a model a question with genuinely insufficient information and a good
answer says so. Most fill the gap with plausible fiction. A task where the
*correct* response is "this cannot be determined from the source provided" is
a strong probe — and healthcare ops is full of real situations like that.

### 2.8 Long-output degradation

Constraints given at the start decay by paragraph six. Ask for something long
*and* constrained and models drift — losing format, contradicting an earlier
claim, dropping a required element.

**A calibration point for your tasks:** studies of LLMs in clinical decision
support estimate hallucination rates of 8–20%. Models fail here often, and
plausibly. Your job is to make that failure *legible and verifiable*, not to
find something they can't do at all.

## Part 3 — The difficulty sweet spot

The email warns against both extremes: don't ask for private data the model
can't have, don't be vague. Here's the operational test.

**A task is too easy when:**
- One retrievable, stable, widely-documented fact answers it
- The answer hasn't changed in years
- A competent search returns it as the top result

**A task is unfair or broken when:**
- It needs private, internal, or real-time data the model has no access to
- No verifiable ground truth exists
- It's ambiguous enough that several answers are defensible
- You yourself can't state the correct answer with confidence

**A task is in the sweet spot when all four hold:**

1. **Verifiable** — there's a right answer, and you can point to the source
2. **Reasoning-dependent** — retrieval alone won't get there; it needs
  composition, comparison, temporal logic, or exclusion
3. **Realistic** — a genuine healthcare ops person would actually need this
4. **Discriminating** — a strong response and a weak one look clearly different

That fourth criterion is the one people miss. If a good answer and a bad answer
are hard to tell apart, the task is useless for training regardless of how
hard it is.

### Building one, mechanically

1. Pick a real workflow from your own experience (compliance review,
  interoperability assessment, audit prep, vendor evaluation)
2. Identify where it depends on something from Part 2 — a status distinction,
  a rule interaction, a jurisdictional overlay
3. Draft the scenario with enough specificity that the answer is determinate
4. **Write the correct answer yourself, with sources, before finalizing.** If
  you can't, the task isn't ready
5. Ask: could a strong model get this right by pattern-matching? If yes,
  tighten it

Step 4 is the discipline that separates good task writers from bad ones. It
also protects you — you can't accidentally submit an unanswerable task.

Part 4 — Instruction compliance

The stated single most common auto-fail. Treat it as a mechanical process, not
a matter of care.

**Before writing anything:**

- Read the full prompt once for meaning
- Read it again and physically list every constraint — count, format, length,
 structure, tone, inclusions, exclusions
- Write to the list
- Check against the list before submitting

The traps specifically:

- "Minimum two sentences" — the email says provide *exactly* two.
When a minimum and an example conflict, follow the most literal reading, and don't
 pad. Their phrasing suggests they treat overshoot as non-compliance.
- Counts mean counts. Three examples means three, not "three or four."
- Format words are literal. "Bulleted list" is not a paragraph. "One paragraph"
 is not two.
- Negative constraints ("do not mention X") are as binding as positive ones and
 easier to violate accidentally.
- If instructions genuinely conflict, follow the more specific one and note the
 conflict briefly rather than silently picking.

**Do a final compliance pass separate from your quality pass.** Different mode
of attention. Reading for quality hides format errors; reading for format
catches them.

Part 5 — Domain refresher: current state, verified

These are checked as of September 2026 with sources. They're useful two ways:
as material for your tasks, and because several are things models get wrong —
which makes them natural task foundations.

**Verify anything you build on. Regulations move, and a stale claim in your
submission reads as carelessness.**

CMS Interoperability & Prior Authorization (CMS-0057-F)

- Released 17 January 2024
- Applies to Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP, and QHP issuers on the FFE
- **1 January 2026** — operational provisions took effect: faster prior auth
 turnaround, specific denial reasons, Patient Access API metric reporting.
 *This deadline has passed.* Non-compliant plans are currently out of
 compliance.
- **1 January 2027** — four production FHIR APIs required

The two-deadline structure is a genuine trap. Models routinely conflate them
or place both in 2027.

ASTP/ONC — HTI rules and TEFCA

HTI-1** finalized December 2023
HTI-2** finalized 16 December 2024 — notably *scaled back* from what was
 proposed, to developer disappointment. Created the **TEFCA Manner Exception**
 to the information blocking rules.
- **HTI-5 proposed rule, 29 December 2025** — proposes **removing** the TEFCA
 Manner Exception. ASTP/ONC's reasoning: it's being misapplied in ways that
 harm exchange, and TEFCA has matured enough not to need the incentive. The
 same rule would scale back certification criteria for AI "decision support
 interventions."

This is a strong seam. A model will likely describe the Manner Exception as
settled law — it was finalized, then targeted for removal within a year.

HIPAA Security Rule overhaul — still not final

- Proposed 27 December 2024, published 6 January 2025, comments closed
 7 March 2025
- Would mandate: encryption of ePHI at rest and in transit (removing
 "addressable"), MFA for all systems touching ePHI, 72-hour incident
 reporting, annual penetration testing, tighter business associate oversight
- **Status as of 2026: still proposed.** The spring 2026 target passed with no
 final rule. HHS moved it to the Long-Term Actions agenda with **July 2027**
 as anticipated final action.
- Over 100 health systems and associations — including Cleveland Clinic, Yale
 New Haven, Advocate Health, the AMA and the AAP — formally asked HHS to
 withdraw it

Models very often present these as current requirements. They are not.

State healthcare AI law

- 177 pending bills across 31 states (NCSL tracker)
- **Texas TRAIGA** — signed June 2025, effective 1 January 2026. Requires
 conspicuous written disclosure to patients when AI is used in diagnosis or
 treatment. **SB 1188** additionally requires practitioners to review
 AI-generated output before clinical decisions.
- **California** — disclaimers required on AI-generated patient communications
 concerning clinical information, plus clear instructions for reaching a
 human clinician
- **Maryland** — disclosure and transparency duties including quarterly
 reporting to the insurance commissioner

Recurring themes across states: transparency to patients, human oversight of
adverse determinations, constraints on AI in prior authorization and claims.

This area changes monthly. Perfect for temporal-currency tasks; verify before
relying on any specific provision.

Part 6 — Sounding like yourself

A human reviewer will read your submission. Some tells that trigger "this is
AI" suspicion, worth avoiding even though your work will be your own:

- Tricolon everywhere — three parallel items, relentlessly
- "It's not just X, it's Y" constructions
- Openings that restate the question before answering
- Uniform paragraph lengths and flawless parallel structure
- Hedging blankets: "it's important to note," "generally speaking," "in many cases"
- Tidy summary paragraphs that add nothing
- Em-dashes used the way I use them

What reads as human expertise: specific numbers, named systems, a concrete
recollection of how something actually works in practice, willingness to say
"in my experience this is usually where it breaks," uneven sentence rhythm,
and occasional plain bluntness. Your HIT and compliance background is a real
asset here — use specifics only someone who's done the work would reach for.

Part 7 — Practice drills

No answers supplied. That's the point.

Drill A — Instruction compliance (20 min)

Find any three prompts with fussy formatting requirements — job application
questions, grant forms, whatever. Extract every constraint into a checklist,
respond, then audit against the checklist. Count your misses. Most people miss
one in three on the first pass, which is exactly why this is the top auto-fail.

Drill B — Break a model, deliberately (60 min)

Open Gemini or ChatGPT. Using Part 5's facts as your ground truth, ask
questions in your own domain where you already know the answer. Log:

- What it got wrong
- Whether the error was confident or hedged
- Whether the failure was retrieval, reasoning, or currency

You're building a personal catalogue of failure patterns. This is legitimate
preparation — your email explicitly recommends it.

Drill C — Task design reps (90 min)

Generate ten task ideas from your own work history. For each, score 1–5 on the
four sweet-spot criteria in Part 3. Keep the ones scoring 4+ on all four. Write
the correct answer, with sources, for your best three.

You'll likely discard six or seven. That's the process working — the discard
rate is what makes the survivors good.

Drill D — Grounded writing (45 min)

Take one Part 5 topic. Write 400 words explaining it to a healthcare ops
manager, with citations. Then verify every factual claim against a primary
source. Note anything you asserted from memory that turned out shaky — that
instinct is what you'll need under time pressure.
